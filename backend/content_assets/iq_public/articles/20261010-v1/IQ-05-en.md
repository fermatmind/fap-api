## Quick Answer

The four-week schedule below is an editorial practice example that can be adjusted or stopped. It is not a validated intelligence-enhancement, educational intervention, or clinical program. Question counts, accuracy, and times in the example table are fictional illustrations, not user or research results.

After an online IQ test, the most controllable growth goal is not a promise to "raise IQ," but improving observation, validation, time management, and error reviews in matrix and visual reasoning. First classify errors as missed conditions, premature assumptions, insufficient rule validation, time allocation, or effects of your state. Then compare process metrics through four weeks of practice. Becoming proficient at one kind of question may improve similar-task performance, but cannot guarantee corresponding improvements in overall intelligence or real-world ability.

## Clarify Goals Before Training

"I want to increase my IQ" is too broad and may create unnecessary identity pressure. Change the goal to observable behaviors:

- Can I enumerate visual changes more completely?
- Can I validate every row and column with the same rule?
- Do I spend less unproductive time on a single question?
- Can I explain why I rule out an answer?
- Are accuracy and completion time more stable at similar difficulty?

These goals relate directly to the task, can be recorded, and allow results that do not support the original hypothesis.

## Build an Error Classification

After each practice session, record only the error mechanism, not "I am unintelligent."

### Missed Conditions

Signs: overlooking direction, quantity, position, or question constraints.

Practice: Begin each question by checking shape, quantity, direction, position, combination, and elimination in a fixed order.

### Premature Assumptions

Signs: stopping the search after seeing one local pattern.

Practice: Write at least two candidate patterns, then eliminate them using all rows and columns.

### Insufficient Validation

Signs: an answer "looks right," but cannot explain other cells.

Practice: Describe the rule in one sentence and explain how it covers every known position.

### Unbalanced Time Allocation

Signs: spending too long on difficult questions and leaving many later ones blank, or making simple errors in pursuit of speed.

Practice: Use checkpoints to ask whether the current approach is producing new information. If it is not, revise the candidate rules or stop repeating an unproductive step. Skipping, marking, and returning apply only to external practice that explicitly permits them; they are not claims about FermatMind's controls.

### Effects of State and Environment

Signs: fatigue, anxiety, noise, or screen display reduces attention.

Practice: Record conditions and compare stable and disrupted environments, without attributing every difference to ability.

Use only materials you have lawfully obtained and are permitted to practise with. Do not use a formal assessment question bank for repeated score practice.

## Week 1: Keep Only an Error Log

The goal is to establish a baseline, not increase the score.

Choose a small number of different questions each time and record:

- Question type;
- Initial assumption;
- Final choice;
- Whether it was correct;
- Time taken;
- Error category;
- Smallest change identified through review.

Do not immediately repeat the same question to prove you know it. Remembering an answer and understanding a pattern are different things.

At the end of Week 1, count the two most common error categories. Train only one of them the following week.

## Week 2: Practice One Strategy

If premature assumptions are the main issue, use "two candidate rules" in every exercise:

1. Write candidate A;
2. Write candidate B;
3. Find a cell that distinguishes them;
4. Then select the answer.

If missed conditions are the main issue, use a fixed scanning checklist. One strategy makes it easier to assess whether changes relate to it, rather than changing speed, question volume, tools, and environment simultaneously.

Alongside accuracy, track the proportion of questions in which you use the strategy. Even if scores remain unchanged for now, more consistent strategy execution may be meaningful progress.

### An Original Example: Test a Rule Against a Counterexample

This label-card exercise was written for this article. It is not taken from FermatMind's or another assessment's question bank and supplies no answers to their items. Each card contains a set of distinct labels. For this exercise, restrict the candidates to rule A: combine the labels from both cards, keeping each once; and rule B: keep only labels that occur on exactly one card.

The first supplied example is left card "tea", right card "book", result "tea, book". Both A and B fit. Selecting A from this example alone would be premature.

Now check a second supplied example: left "tea, book", right "book", result "tea". A predicts "tea, book" and conflicts with the result. B removes the shared label "book" and explains both examples. Among the two stipulated candidates, choose B. The useful step is identifying which observation rejected A, rather than memorizing a general instruction to remove matching labels.

Try unused materials: left "blue, yellow", right "yellow, green". Predict "blue, green" with B, then check the supplied result, which is "blue, green". Also test left "blue" and right "blue": B predicts an empty card, matching the supplied empty result. These check partial and complete overlap within this invented exercise.

If a new task instead specifies that matching labels remain, so two "blue" cards produce "blue", B fails and A should be reconsidered. If neither candidate explains the known conditions, check for a missed label or task instruction and expand the candidates. Do not force the removal rule onto the task. A few examples cannot eliminate every possible rule.

A useful log is: "Both candidates fit the first example; the second rejects A; B fits the unused examples here; changed conditions require new candidates." Answering the original example faster may only show recall. Explaining a rule in similar new materials provides a different observation.

## Week 3: Compare Timed and Untimed Practice

Choose two sets of questions as similar as possible in difficulty and number:

- One without strict timing, focusing on complete reasoning;
- One with reasonable time checkpoints; practise skipping and returning only if the external materials explicitly allow it, otherwise follow their required order.

Compare:

- Accuracy differences;
- Median time per item, only when complete item-level timing is available;
- Error types;
- Whether time pressure increases premature assumptions;
- Which question types are worth doing first and which should be skipped temporarily.

The goal is not blindly speeding up, but finding a balance between speed and accuracy.

## Week 4: Mixed Questions and Transfer Checks

Mix different patterns to avoid relying only on familiar templates. Afterward, ask:

1. Can I genuinely explain the rule?
2. Do scanning and validation steps remain effective with a new format?
3. Does improvement occur only on practiced questions, or transfer to similar new ones?
4. Which strategy costs too much to retain?

Finally, retain one or two habits supported by your observations, such as "scan completely before generating a rule" or "at a checkpoint, ask whether this approach is still producing new information." If the target errors persist in new materials, or the checks take substantially longer without producing clearer reasons, simplify the steps, try another strategy, or stop the current practice.

## Prevent Training from Becoming Score Anxiety

Set limits: a fixed number of weekly sessions and a fixed session length, without adding practice after one low score. Review strategies before looking at numbers. If several consecutive sessions become only an attempt to prove yourself rather than learn new methods, pause for a week.

You can define success as "I validated the rule through the process," "I reassessed my approach at the checkpoint," or "I can describe the error mechanism." These indicators still relate to question types but are more controllable than pursuing one total score. Do not make public leaderboard-style comparisons with others, or turn practice scores into self-esteem, parent-child rewards, or work evaluations.

If parents, teachers, or teams initiate training, participants should be allowed to refuse public score disclosure, with advance explanation of data use. Ordinary practice must not quietly become a selection tool. Feedback should address strategies and processes, without identity judgments such as "smart," "stupid," or "lacking talent."

## How to Record Process Metrics

A simple table is recommended:

| Date | Question count | Accuracy | Median time | Most common error | Strategy execution rate | State |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---|---:|---|
| Example | 10 | 70% | 75 seconds | Premature assumptions | 60% | Average sleep |

When comparing trends, keep question volume, difficulty, and environment as comparable as possible. Do not draw conclusions from too few observations.

Here, median time means the middle item time after sorting; for an even number of items, average the two middle values. Total duration divided by item count is a mean, not a median, and cannot reconstruct item-level records. If your external practice has no separate item timing, leave the median blank and record total session duration separately. Define strategy execution rate as items with the agreed steps completed divided by applicable items with records; state both the steps and the denominator before comparing sessions.

## Near Transfer Is Not an Increase in Overall Intelligence

Separate three observations. Faster answers to the same items may reflect answer memory. On structurally similar but previously unused items, explaining the rule and making fewer target errors gives you a reason to examine whether the strategy is usable in new materials. On an unfamiliar learning or work task, define and observe that task's actual goal separately; matrix accuracy is not a measure of its outcome.

In research, near transfer generally concerns untrained tasks similar to training, whereas far transfer concerns more different tasks. An individual's before-and-after gain does not by itself establish near transfer, a training cause, or increased general intelligence. Difficulty, state, familiarity, and other learning may also have changed. The [Simons and colleagues review](https://doi.org/10.1177/1529100616661983) discusses the design and claim boundaries of brain-training studies; it does not validate this four-week schedule.

The [Melby-Lervåg and colleagues meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1177/1745691616635612) concerns working-memory training. Its tasks and comparison conditions constrain its conclusions. It cannot validate the matrix practice here or establish that all learning is ineffective.

Therefore, this page does not promise:

- A fixed IQ increase in four weeks;
- Surpassing a particular number of people after training;
- Better school, income, or career outcomes;
- Treating cognitive or health problems through practice.

Growth can be real, but conclusions must stay consistent with the behaviors measured.

## When to Pause

- Repeated practice causes clear anxiety or self-disparagement;
- Pursuing numbers sacrifices sleep, study, or work;
- Repeated use of the same question bank only memorizes answers;
- Formal assessment is needed, but online practice keeps replacing it;
- Sudden or persistent cognitive changes or health concerns occur.

When formal conclusions are needed, use a qualified assessment appropriate to the purpose. For health concerns, seek suitable professional support.

## Review One Real Learning Task

This is an invented review suggestion, not a research validation protocol or proof of far transfer from matrix practice. Suppose that after reading a textbook paragraph, you often mistake its example for its definition. Choose a short, unfamiliar passage, read it as usual, close the book, and write the definition and one example in your own words. Check the source for missing necessary conditions and record the specific omissions, without converting them into an IQ claim.

For another new passage of roughly comparable difficulty, add just one step: list the necessary conditions and construct a similar-looking counterexample that fails one of them. Close the book, explain the concept again, and check the source. Record omissions and completion time. If omissions decrease, tentatively retain the habit and observe it in further new passages. This remains a personal learning log and does not establish that the step caused improvement.

If it does not help, locate the failure. For unfamiliar terms, clarify their meanings or ask a teacher. If you understand while reading but cannot explain with the book closed, use a smaller passage and try recalling it before checking. If counterexamples consume too much time, work on one core concept. Faster answers after rereading the same passage do not by themselves demonstrate understanding, and keeping a log should not displace rest or ordinary study.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can Four Weeks of Training Raise IQ?

This four-week schedule has not been validated for effectiveness and does not guarantee an IQ increase. You can record strategy use, specific errors, and performance on similar new items, but those personal observations do not by themselves establish transfer, a training cause, or increased overall intelligence.

### How Long Should I Practice Each Day?

Use small amounts that do not noticeably affect study, work, or rest. A fixed frequency and high-quality reviews are usually easier to observe than large amounts of practice in one session.

### Should I Repeat the Same Test?

Using the same question bank to demonstrate improvement is not recommended because memory and familiarity effects interfere with judgments. Use lawfully obtained, permitted practice with similar but unused materials, and record conditions and reasoning. Better performance on new materials still does not establish transfer or increased intelligence from a personal before-and-after comparison alone.

### If My Score Does Not Improve, Has Training Failed?

Not necessarily. First examine error types, strategy execution, and consistency. If these also do not improve, adjust strategies or stop rather than blaming yourself.

### Can Children or Students Follow This Training?

This article presents general learning strategies, not an educational or clinical program. Guardians and appropriate education professionals should choose tools for minors based on actual needs. Online results must not be used to label children's ability.
