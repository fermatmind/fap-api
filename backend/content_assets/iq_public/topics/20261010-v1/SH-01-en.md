# IQ and EQ: Differences, Test Choices, and Reading Results

IQ and EQ address different questions. Cognitive tests observe cognitive performance, such as reasoning, through specific tasks. Tools related to emotional intelligence concern the recognition, understanding, and regulation of emotional information and related behavior, but may use ability tasks, self-report, or situational judgments; their results cannot be used interchangeably.

FermatMind's two current entry points provide information from different sources: the original 30-question visual reasoning exercise records performance in one response session; the 60-item EQ self-report organizes your descriptions of emotional and relational tendencies. The two numbers cannot be compared to determine who scores higher or is better, nor combined into a stable personality type or career ability level.

## Compare methods before choosing a tool

| Question to compare | FermatMind visual reasoning exercise | FermatMind EQ-60 self-report |
|---|---|---|
| What are you doing? | Comparing visual relationships and choosing answers | Describing how you usually feel and act |
| Where does this session's information come from? | Performance on this set of questions | Your self-report in this session |
| Scope | 30 original visual matrix tasks | Four dimensions: Self-Awareness, Emotion Regulation, Empathy, and Relationship Management |
| How should scores be read first? | Number correct and accuracy; understand Beta separately according to its simulated source | First confirm the fields and references, then consider the four dimensions, response quality, and real situations |
| What cannot be directly inferred? | All aspects of intelligence, a stable ability type, or a career ceiling | Actual communication ability, other people's feelings, work performance, or relationship outcomes |
| What is a more useful next step? | Test your habit of checking rules on new material | Test your self-understanding through specific events and the other person's confirmation |

EQ-60's four dimensions form its current interpretation framework: Self-Awareness concerns your emotions and their triggers; Emotion Regulation concerns pausing, recovering, and choosing a response; Empathy concerns other people's possible feelings and perspectives; Relationship Management concerns expression, collaboration, boundaries, and repair. It is not MSCEIT or a validated ability test. Current content evidence is preliminary and norms are provisional; internal consistency, retesting, structure, external associations, and equivalence across languages and groups still require validation.

Self-report has value for reflection, but “I think I can understand others” and “I actually understood this person on this occasion” are different kinds of evidence. Empathy requires checking your guesses; relationship management can also include refusal and clear boundaries, and does not mean accommodating others at all costs.

## Similar-looking scores may have completely different references

In the visual exercise, 18/30 = 60% means only the proportion of answers correct. The published Beta calculation uses a randomly simulated baseline and cannot be treated as population IQ or a percentile, nor does it guarantee that Beta is displayed in the current report interface.

If EQ includes POMP, it represents a position within the scale's theoretical possible score range, not “how many people you scored higher than”. For example, in a purely hypothetical teaching example, a scale ranges from 20–100 and the score is 80, giving a range position of (80−20)÷(100−20)×100% = 75%; this is not the percentile 75, nor an explanation of this site's current formula.

Standard scores and percentiles require separate examination of the actual reference, version, and status. An EQ standard score is not IQ; provisional references must not be interpreted as representative population rankings without verification. Do not add or subtract the two scores or use them to select people.

## One delayed project may involve two different problems

The following is a synthetic scenario, not user data, a test item, or evidence of an effect.

During a project review, Lin discovers that the team estimated three sequential tasks—first confirming requirements, then production, and finally acceptance—as parallel tasks, making the schedule too short. Meanwhile, after Lin says, “Your estimate was wrong,” a colleague stops contributing information. Lin has always considered themself good at understanding others, but has not yet confirmed what this silence means.

The first step is to describe the observations clearly: the dependencies in the schedule are wrong; the other person stopped explaining during the conversation. The former requires checking task assumptions, dependencies, and durations; the latter requires checking your reactions, expression, and guesses about the other person. A calculation error cannot justify labeling a colleague “low IQ”, nor can silence establish “low EQ”.

The second step is to look for alternative explanations. An estimation error may arise from missing requirements, insufficient professional knowledge, or time pressure; silence may reflect fatigue, a need to think, power differences, or concern about being blamed. Different causes require different adjustments, and test scores cannot replace evidence from the situation.

The third step is to address the problems together. First mark which tasks must be completed in sequence and which conditions remain unknown, then ask: “Are you most concerned about the schedule, resources, or responsibility arrangements? It could also be something else.” Let the colleague correct your guesses, confirm them, and then agree on one specific adjustment, such as reallocating acceptance time or identifying the person responsible for requirements. Understanding the difficulty does not require Lin to promise additional work they cannot take on.

The fourth step is to review: can the new schedule withstand checks of dependencies and counterexamples, does the other person confirm that the problem was accurately understood, and are responsibilities clearer? If there is no improvement, reexamine resources, authority, task definition, or communication timing; do not simply repeat tests. Where humiliation, retaliation, or safety risks are present, do not require the person involved to maintain the conversation by showing higher “emotional intelligence”; turn to appropriate support and channels.

## Choose a reading path based on your current question

### 1. I want to try a tool

To experience visual rule judgments, start with the [original 30-question visual reasoning exercise](/en/tests/iq-test-intelligence-quotient-assessment). To organize your self-perceptions about emotions and relationships, start with the [60-item emotional and relational self-report](/en/tests/eq-test-emotional-intelligence-assessment). Choose according to what you want to observe, rather than which tool might give you a higher number.

### 2. I want to understand a score

For IQ-related results, start with [Intelligence test scores and limits](/en/articles/iq-test-score-and-limits-explained), distinguishing accuracy, Beta, and population references. For EQ, first read the [60-item emotional and relational self-report entry](/en/tests/eq-test-emotional-intelligence-assessment), confirming its four dimensions, self-report identity, and scoring explanation. When you encounter percentiles or standard scores, check the reference explanations in the actual report; POMP cannot replace them.

### 3. I want to understand concepts and measurement scope

Read the [FermatMind intelligence test guide](/en/articles/iq-test-tool-guide) to understand the difference between this site's visual tasks and a comprehensive cognitive assessment. Read the [60-item emotional and relational self-report entry](/en/tests/eq-test-emotional-intelligence-assessment) to check the self-report method and use boundaries, then use this page's comparison to understand how it differs from ability tasks. These are existing reading entry points at the tool level; this page already provides the core answer that these two kinds of information cannot be exchanged.

### 4. I want to practice an observable action

For reasoning, read [Intelligence test practice and review](/en/articles/iq-test-growth-guide), starting with checking rules and counterexamples. For emotions and relationships, use this page's delayed-project example to ground reflection in a specific event: name a feeling, confirm the other person's concern, and make a clear request or state a boundary. Review using new material and real feedback, rather than substituting rising test scores for actual change.

### 5. I want to use a question to review my work

First use this page's delayed-project example to review a real task, recording dependencies, resource conditions, expression, and colleague feedback separately, then choose one observable adjustment. Neither of this site's results is used for recruitment, promotion, performance appraisal, or job screening, nor do they prove career success. At work, first ask about task requirements and resource conditions, then seek corresponding evidence.

## Why there is no universal ratio for which matters more

Reasoning and emotion-related activities may both play a part in real situations; they cannot simply be divided into “intelligence handles tasks, emotional intelligence handles people”. The evidence a problem requires depends on the task and situation. There is no universal ratio claiming “success depends 20% on IQ and 80% on EQ”, nor can a person's probability of success be derived from this site's two scores.

For the distinction between ability measurement and self-report in emotional intelligence research, see [Mayer, Salovey, and Caruso's theoretical review](https://aec6905spring2013.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/01/mayersaloveycaruso-2004.pdf). It provides a distinction between methods and does not transfer the validity of other tools to EQ-60. For matching score interpretations to uses, see the [Standards for Educational and Psychological Testing](https://www.testingstandards.net/uploads/7/6/6/4/76643089/standards_2014edition.pdf).
