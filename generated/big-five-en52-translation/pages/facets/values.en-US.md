# Values Facet: How Readily Do You Reexamine Established Beliefs?

Values concerns willingness to examine and reevaluate established beliefs, traditions, and social norms. It describes a way of relating to beliefs—not which political, religious, ethical, or personal conclusions someone holds.

## Precise Definition

When encountering “this is how it has always been done,” a stronger tendency asks why, where the rule came from, and whether it still fits. A weaker tendency may give established answers, continuity, or inherited frameworks more initial weight.

Neither pattern determines morality or ideology. A person may examine tradition and deliberately retain it, or accept a currently progressive norm without much reflection. The process and the conclusion must be separated.

## Parent Domain

Values belongs to [Openness to Experience](/en/personality/big-five/openness) in the NEO/IPIP-NEO vocabulary used by this package. The page does not claim that this is the only possible structure across all Openness measures.

## Distinguishing Neighboring Facets

- **Values versus Ideas:** [Ideas](/en/personality/big-five/facets/ideas) concerns interest in thinking; Values specifically brings norms and personal commitments under review.
- **Values versus Actions:** [Actions](/en/personality/big-five/facets/actions) broadens lived activities. Novel behavior does not necessarily revise a belief.
- **Values versus rebellion:** Rejecting authority can have many motives. Values concerns examination, which may end in retention, revision, or uncertainty.

## Behavioral Cues

A stronger tendency may involve tracing where beliefs came from, considering several cultural frameworks, changing a view after new evidence, or distinguishing “common” from “right.” A weaker tendency may involve trusting time-tested rules, preferring clearer moral reference points, and maintaining stable commitments. Public speech may reflect safety and conformity pressures more than private examination.

## Contextual Differences

Pluralistic settings may make belief review visible. Homogeneous or punitive settings may keep it private. Life transitions, migration, education, family responsibility, and direct experience can also activate reevaluation. One controversial opinion cannot establish the broader tendency.

## Possible Expressions When Stronger

- Naming the source and assumptions behind a belief.
- Trying to understand the logic of an opposing worldview.
- Revising a position without treating revision as weakness.
- Defining a good life through personally examined commitments.
- Remaining uncertain longer when several value systems have coherent reasons.

## Possible Expressions When Weaker

- Treating some questions as sufficiently settled.
- Maintaining a stable reference framework across adulthood.
- Giving family, tradition, community, or established consensus substantial weight.
- Preferring clearer boundaries over prolonged moral ambiguity.
- Finding rapid cultural change harder to integrate.

## Strengths and Costs

Stronger Values can support adaptation across cultures and deliberate self-renewal. Possible costs include indecision, appearing uncommitted, or drifting into indiscriminate relativism.

Weaker Values can support continuity, identity coherence, and decisive action under a trusted framework. Possible costs include slower adaptation or insufficient review when circumstances change. Neither tendency proves open-mindedness in every domain, virtue, prejudice, courage, or conformity.

## An Original Case

A woman reviews expectations inherited from a traditional family one by one. She retains respect for elders, revises assumptions about women’s roles, and can explain why. Her pattern is neither blanket rebellion nor obedience; the relevant feature is deliberate examination.

## An Original Counterexample

A young man holds strong mainstream values but rarely asks where they came from. When friends disagree, he reports that his own framework simply feels settled. His conclusions alone do not identify the facet; the limited examination process is the relevant clue.

## Self-Observation Questions

1. Can you name several central values and where they came from?
2. When meeting an opposing worldview, do you first judge, investigate, or disengage?
3. Which belief changed in the past five years, and what prompted the change?
4. Which questions feel settled beyond useful review?
5. When everyone follows a practice, do you distinguish prevalence from justification?

## Low-Risk Action Experiment

Choose three beliefs you hold and trace each to family, experience, community, evidence, or a specific influence. Then write the strongest fair reason for one position you reject. The purpose is to observe examination, not force a new conclusion or weaken a valued commitment.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Does strong Values mean having no principles?**

No. Someone may hold clear principles after examining and choosing them.

**Does weaker Values mean being backward or conservative?**

No. This facet is not a moral or political label. Any current position can be held with more or less ongoing examination.

**Can Values predict political ideology or voting?**

No individual ideology or vote follows from this page, and the package provides no such product-level predictive evidence.

**Can someone train greater openness in Values?**

Exposure and perspective-taking may expand behavior. This page does not promise a stable trait change.

**Does disagreeing with family prove strong Values?**

No. Disagreement can arise from many causes. The facet concerns the process of examining beliefs, not merely reaching a different conclusion.
