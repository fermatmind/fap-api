## How to solve a matrix reasoning question

A matrix reasoning question asks you to infer a relationship between cells and apply it to a missing cell. A useful approach is to separate the features, propose a rule, check it against another completed row or column, and only then compare the options. A rule that explains one row by coincidence is not enough.

The three worked examples below were created for this article. They teach ways to check a pattern; they are not answer explanations for FermatMind's test bank. They cannot cover every possible matrix rule.

If you want to try the full practice form, the [free 30-question IQ test and free results](/en/tests/iq-test-intelligence-quotient-assessment) use original visual reasoning questions. Allow about 20 minutes. That is an estimated duration, not a countdown limit established by this guide. This is reasoning practice, rather than a professional intelligence assessment.

## A four-step method before you choose an answer

1. **Describe the features separately.** Count the objects, then inspect direction, position, shape and fill. “It looks similar” does not identify a relationship.
2. **State one candidate rule.** For example, “one circle is added at each step to the right.” A precise statement gives you something to test.
3. **Try to disprove it.** Check another completed row or column. If the rule predicts a different known cell, reject it instead of changing it just to preserve your first guess.
4. **Predict the missing cell before comparing options.** Check every relevant feature. An option can have the right direction and the wrong number of objects.

Not every puzzle uses the same relationship across rows and down columns. Check the relationships the given cells support; do not invent a second rule just because the grid has two directions.

## Example 1: count the circles and reject a tempting rule

All the circles in this example have the same shape, size and fill.

| Row | First cell | Second cell | Third cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ○ | ○○ | ○○○ |
| 2 | ○○ | ○○○ | ○○○○ |
| 3 | ○○○ | ○○○○ | ? |

Choose from A: four circles; B: five circles; C: six circles; D: five triangles. Pause before reading the explanation.

**Answer: B, five circles.** Each step to the right adds one circle. The same increase also holds down each column, so both checks predict five circles in the missing cell.

The tempting rule “add the first two counts to get the third” fits row 1: 1 + 2 = 3. It fails in row 2, where 2 + 3 would predict five rather than the four circles actually shown. A rule must account for the known cells, not just one convenient example. D matches the required count but changes the shape without support.

## Example 2: track direction and dot count independently

The arrow turns clockwise by a quarter turn at each step to the right. Test that proposal against the grid rather than assuming it is correct. A bullet, •, represents one dot.

| Row | First cell | Second cell | Third cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ↑ • | → •• | ↓ ••• |
| 2 | → • | ↓ •• | ← ••• |
| 3 | ↓ • | ← •• | ? |

Options: A: ↑ •; B: ↑ •••; C: → •••; D: ↓ •••.

**Answer: B, an upward arrow and three dots.** In row 3, down → left → up continues the clockwise quarter turns. The other two rows follow the same rule. Down each column, the arrow also advances by one quarter turn. The dot count follows a separate relationship: one dot in column 1, two in column 2 and three in column 3.

A gets the direction right but ignores the dot count. C and D get the count right but fail the arrow rule. Splitting the features makes it possible to identify exactly why an option fails, instead of judging the whole picture at once.

## Example 3: combine symbols, then remove the repeated symbol

In this example, each row combines its first two cells using the same operation. The order of symbols inside a cell does not matter. Compare three candidate operations: keep all symbols, keep only shared symbols, or keep only symbols appearing in exactly one of the first two cells.

| Row | First cell | Second cell | Third cell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | ○ △ | △ □ | ○ □ |
| 2 | ◇ ○ | ○ ☆ | ◇ ☆ |
| 3 | △ ☆ | ☆ □ | ? |

Options: A: △ ☆ □; B: ☆; C: △ □; D: ○ □.

**Answer: C, triangle and square, under the operation demonstrated by the completed rows.** In row 1, the triangle occurs in both starting cells and disappears; the circle and square each occur once and remain. Row 2 checks the operation: the repeated circle disappears, leaving diamond and star. In row 3, remove the repeated star and retain triangle and square.

A keeps the repeated star, as an “all symbols” operation would. B keeps only the shared star. Neither operation explains the completed rows. D copies row 1's result without using row 3's inputs. This is a teaching example of one combination rule, not a claim that every matrix question uses it or that a finite grid can prove a unique rule among all imaginable rules.

## What to do when none of these rules fits

Return to the features you can actually see. Check whether you missed a change in position, fill or direction, or whether two relationships operate independently. Compare a proposed rule with a completed part of the problem before applying it to the blank. If several options still seem possible, keep that uncertainty; adding an arbitrary exception is not evidence for a solution.

After practice, record one specific observation, such as “I checked the direction but missed the dot count.” A total score cannot recover that observation for you. The [guide to reviewing one reasoning-test session](/en/articles/iq-test-narrative-portrait) explains how to distinguish your own notes from conclusions a score cannot support.

## Turn the examples into a useful practice session

Use new material rather than repeatedly recalling the answers above. For each problem, write the proposed rule, a known cell that checks it, and the feature that rejects a tempting option. On a later problem, see whether that checking step catches an error you otherwise would have missed.

You can change the counts or starting directions in these examples to create a new exercise. Recalculate the full grid and the options; changing a symbol without checking the consequences can produce an ambiguous or inconsistent question. Correctly solving a self-made example is practice in applying that rule, not a standardized assessment.

For a longer routine, follow the [four-week reasoning practice plan](/en/articles/iq-test-growth-guide). Retest research finds that repeated administration can affect cognitive-test performance; it does not validate a particular improvement claim for this practice form. A higher score on familiar material alone does not establish an increase in general intelligence. See [Scharfen, Peters and Holling's retest meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.intell.2018.01.003).

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a matrix reasoning test the same as a complete IQ assessment?

No. Matrix questions focus on relationships in visual patterns. A broader cognitive assessment can include other tasks and requires evidence for its intended interpretation. FermatMind's original 30-question online form is reasoning practice and does not replace professional assessment.

### Are the FermatMind test and results both free?

Yes. The current original 30-question test and its results are free. Allow about 20 minutes; actual time varies. The worked examples in this article are separate teaching material, not answers to the test bank.

### Should I always add the shapes or rotate them?

No. Those are possible relationships, not universal rules. Check any proposed operation against the completed cells. A rule that fails a known row should be rejected, even if an answer option looks plausible.

### Do these examples reveal the answers to the real test?

No. They were created for this article to demonstrate checking a rule, separating features and rejecting an option. They are not the test bank's answer explanations and do not cover every question type.

### Does a better practice score mean my IQ increased?

Not by itself. Familiarity with items and methods can affect retest performance. Record what changed in your approach and conditions, and use new material to check a strategy; a score increase alone does not establish a change in general intelligence.
